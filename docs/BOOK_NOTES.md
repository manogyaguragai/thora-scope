# Book notes — Wells & Coonar, *Thoracic Surgical Techniques* (Springer 2018)

Own paraphrase for teaching an anterior-approach VATS RML lobectomy; not quotations. Page = printed book page
(ch.2 = PDF p.15–20, §31.4 = PDF p.129, ch.32 = PDF p.130–131). Pending clinician review.

## Setup, anaesthesia, team
- Good one-lung isolation is a prerequisite; a coughing patient or an over-inflated lung makes VATS very hard (p2).
- Trend is from a posterior-standing surgeon to an anterior approach; fixed or robotic holders cut the need for an assistant (p3).
- Patient lies operative side up, centred on the table, arm raised above 90°; the operative lung is isolated and suctioned to help it collapse (p5).
- Surgeon and assistant stand anterior, scrub nurse posterior; screens on both sides or one over the head (p5).
- Teams should rehearse urgent conversion, particularly for bleeding, because real conversions get rarer with experience (p4–5).

## Scope and instruments
- 5 or 10 mm telescopes dominate; a 30° angled scope is now the usual choice over a 0° one (p5).
- Steel dissectors and graspers give feel; small gauze pledgets on instruments act as peanut dissectors and sponge-sticks (p5).
- Short angled blunt forceps are the tool for getting around vessels and the bronchus (p5).
- Diathermy tips are insulated except at the very end to avoid collateral injury; sealing devices are not trusted alone on vessels (p5).
- Staplers: 2–3-row loads for vessels and bronchus, 3-row for parenchyma; reinforced cartridges now load quickly (p5).

## Ports (multiport layout used by the authors)
- Utility incision 2–5 cm around the 5th interspace, centred just in front of the anterior axillary line, avoiding breast tissue (p6).
- Camera port ~1 cm around the 8th interspace, just behind the anterior axillary line (p6).
- Optional third (retraction) port of similar size, same level, in the midscapular line; any port may swap roles (p6).
- Local anaesthetic before incision (and again at the end); split rather than cut muscle; take intercostal muscle off the upper rib border (p6).
- A soft-tissue wound protector helps, especially when looking through the utility incision (p6).
- RML specifics: uniportal ~4 cm, or multiport with 2–3 incisions of 1–2 cm (p134).

## Common opening steps
- First confirm the lung is properly down; pressing with a sponge-stick for a few minutes can help (p6).
- Divide adhesions before assessing, otherwise dissection and later re-expansion suffer (p6).
- Survey the cavity and confirm the plan before dissecting (p6).

## Principles shared by all VATS lobectomies (p6–7)
- Core themes: thorough node dissection, phrenic protection, respect for venous variants, target inside the specimen with margin, patency check before stapling, and a plan for incomplete fissures (p6).
- Do node dissection early: it reveals anatomy, avoids skimping at the end and gives time to see haemostasis (p7).
- Right-sided standard stations: 2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10; VATS often works from medial to (underneath) the lung; 4R/2R are reached by lifting the azygos (p7).
- Phrenic protection: open the pleura at the base of the pulmonary vein early and push the phrenic nerve forward; control bleeders there with small clips, not diathermy (p7).
- Review venous anatomy on contrast CT before surgery and recheck during it; the middle lobe is the lobe most at risk of venous infarction (p7).
- Feel the tumour and the removed specimen to confirm the target is out; ask pathology if unsure (p7).

## Patency check before stapling the bronchus
- Classic disasters: dividing the wrong (main) bronchus, and injuring middle lobe venous drainage (p7).
- Sequence: anaesthetist suctions the airway → bronchus clamped → test ventilation, judging both easy inflation and quick deflation; if doubtful, adjust and check bronchoscopically (p7).
- In the RUL chapter the same rule: suction plus brief re-inflation before the stapler closes on the bronchus (p131).

## Fissure-last approach
- Open surgery found the artery in the fissure; VATS does vein → easy artery branches → bronchus → remaining arteries → fissure (p7).
- Benefits: no hunting for the artery in a fused fissure, less fissure dissection, less air leak (p7, p131).
- When only the fissure holds the lobe, lift it and place the stapler; a short ventilation shows the aerated lobe boundary; typically 2–3 fires (p131).

## Anterior-hilum lessons from the RUL section (p131)
- The phrenic nerve lies in front of the superior pulmonary vein; sweep it toward the mediastinum with a peanut.
- Stay on the vein, because the pulmonary artery sits directly behind it.
- Pass an angled clamp to prove the space is fully open before introducing a stapler.
- Before dividing an upper lobe vein, confirm the middle lobe's own drainage is intact.
- Avoid traction on fragile artery branches; push the clamp posteriorly away from the artery when passing it.

## RML anatomy and sequence (ch.32)
- Open view: lung pulled back exposes the lower superior pulmonary vein; the middle lobe vein is followed into the lobe (p133).
- A small interlobar vein lies just above it; do not narrow the upper-lobe division by tying/stapling too proximally — risk of thrombosis and upper lobe infarction (p133).
- The RML bronchus sits directly beneath the vein (p133).
- Enlarged nodes usually lie between the lower edge of the RML bronchus and the front of the lower lobe bronchus (≈11R); bronchial arteries there need coagulation (p133).
- Hug the upper border of the bronchus: the middle lobe artery or arteries lie immediately above it (p133).
- Encircle the bronchus with a right-angle clamp; divide close to (about 5 mm from) its origin off the intermediate bronchus (p133).
- One or two middle lobe arteries become obvious once the bronchus is divided (p133).
- If the fissure is complete, the arteries can be found at the posterior angle of the middle lobe with the posterior segmental and lower-lobe apical branches (p134).
- Stapling across the horizontal fissure tends to stop air leak sooner than traction/cautery separation (p134).
- VATS sequence: identify and open pleura over the vein → encircle and staple → clear nodes around the bronchus → staple the bronchus → staple the 1–2 arteries → complete fissure on both sides → small bag (p134).
- While stapling, keep the bronchus intermedius, lower lobe bronchus and lower lobe arteries clear (p134).
- The anterior approach makes RML one of the simplest VATS lobectomies (p134).

## Finishing and complications
- Bag the specimen to avoid wound seeding (p6, p131, p134).
- Wash out with warm water and check the parenchyma and bronchial stump for leaks; small leaks usually settle (p6, p131).
- Larger leaks: re-staple (possibly reinforced), sealant, talc pleurodesis or a pleural tent (p6).
- Complete the node dissection, secure haemostasis, one apicobasal drain via the uniportal or lowest port (p131, p134).
- Intercostal blocks can be placed under direct vision at the end (p131).
- Usual discharge after RML VATS: 1–2 days (p134).
